VOICEOVER SCRIPT (English approval draft):

One invisible frame, and your wallet belongs to someone else.
That’s the Vulture Banker’s pitch. Javelina grips his popcorn.
But who decided a movie could control your mind?

Follow that glowing reel back to 1957.
Advertiser James Vicary claimed hidden messages made cinema audiences buy more popcorn and cola.
Messages flashed so quickly, viewers supposedly never noticed them.
A frightening idea: someone could sell you something without your permission.

But a frightening claim still needs convincing evidence.
Where was the independently verified proof that those flashes caused those purchases?
The famous sales claim did not establish that.
A later National Research Council report described Vicary’s study as a hoax.

Yet the story sparked controversy and inspired more investigations.
The claim had become a mystery people wanted answered.
And a scary explanation can travel further than its correction.

Back in town, the Banker has turned that fear into another sales pitch.
He offers Javelina protection from the very reel he’s selling.

Here’s the distinction his pitch leaves out.
Researchers have found subliminal effects on simple tasks under controlled conditions.
That doesn’t establish a secret switch that makes cinema customers obey.

Javelina puts his wallet away. The Banker’s smile slips.
Now that amber glow looks a lot less impressive.
Javelina stamps the original advertisement: MYTH. Then, one satisfied nod.
Case closed. Fear was the product.
